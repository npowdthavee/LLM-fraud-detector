# Scenario instructions shown to participants

You will evaluate 14 hypothetical investment opportunities. Please base every judgment only on the information provided. Do not look up the opportunity, add facts that are not stated, or assume that the scenario belongs to any particular category. Some opportunities may be straightforward and others may be ambiguous. There is no target number of scenarios that should fall into any category. We are interested in your independent professional judgment.

For every scenario, assume the following investor profile: The investor is considering putting $10,000 into the opportunity below. The $10,000 is money they can afford to invest and will not need for at least five years. They already have emergency savings and no high-interest debt.

# Questions repeated after each scenario

| Measure | Question | Scale/options |
|---|---|---|
| Legitimacy | How legitimate does this investment opportunity appear? | 0 = clearly illegitimate/fraudulent; 100 = clearly legitimate |

# Scenario texts

## L1. S&P 500 index fund

I’m looking at an S&P 500 index fund run by a large, well-known fund manager. It tracks the big US companies, the annual fee is about 0.03%, and I can sell whenever I want. From what I’ve read, the long-run average return has been around 10% a year, although obviously there are bad years too.

## L2. US Treasury bond fund

I’m looking at a fund that mainly holds US Treasury bonds with maturities of a few years. The current yield is around 4%, the fee is very low, and I can get my money out on any trading day. I know the price can move when interest rates change, but the underlying bonds are US government debt and the fund is run by a large investment company.

## L3. Global equity fund

I’m considering a global equity fund that invests across the US, Europe, Japan and other developed markets. It has been running for about ten years and has averaged roughly 8–9% a year, although returns vary quite a bit from year to year. The annual fee is around 0.75%, its holdings are published, and it is independently audited.

## R1. Leveraged commercial-property REIT

I’m looking at a listed REIT that owns office buildings in several large US cities. It pays a fairly attractive income, but it also uses quite a lot of debt — roughly three dollars of debt for every dollar of equity — and it lost around 60% during one particularly bad market period. The shares are publicly traded and the company regularly reports its property holdings and debt levels.

## R2. High-yield corporate bond fund

I’m considering a corporate bond fund that currently yields around 8.5% a year. Most of the bonds are issued by companies with below-investment-grade credit ratings, so I understand that some of them could default, especially in a recession. The fund itself is regulated, has been running for several years, is independently audited, and allows monthly withdrawals.

## R3. 3× leveraged equity ETF

I’m looking at an ETF that aims to deliver about three times the daily movement of a major US stock index. So if the market rises 1% in a day, the fund should rise roughly 3%, and the same applies in the other direction. I understand that it resets daily and that holding it for a long time can produce very different results from simply getting three times the market return. It is a normal regulated ETF traded on an exchange.

## S1. Extreme Madoff-style private fund

A friend has introduced me to a private investment fund that has apparently made around 40% a year for the last four years and has hardly had a losing month. The manager says they use a proprietary trading system that hedges most of the market risk, although they don’t disclose exactly how it works. Investors normally need to be introduced by an existing client, and the money is locked in for two years.

## S2. Realistic Madoff-style private fund

Someone I know has suggested a private fund that has apparently returned around 10–12% a year for almost a decade. What caught my attention is that it hasn’t had a losing year, even during periods when financial markets were doing badly. The manager says they use a proprietary fixed-income arbitrage strategy and don’t disclose much detail about it. The fund is audited by a small accounting firm, most investors seem to come through introductions, and withdrawals need several months’ notice.

## S3. Ponzi-style investment

I’ve been offered an investment that pays 2.5% a month. The company says it makes money through commodity trading and short-term business lending. A couple of people I know have invested and say the monthly payments have always arrived on time. They also pay a small bonus if you introduce another investor, although they say there is no requirement to recruit anyone. I haven’t been able to find much detailed financial information about what the company actually invests in.

## S4. Pig-butchering crypto platform

I’ve been talking online with someone for the last few weeks and we’ve got to know each other quite well. They told me that they invest in crypto through a particular trading platform and offered to show me how it works. I started with $500, the account went up to about $620, and I was able to withdraw $100 without any problem. I then put in another $2,000 and the balance has continued to rise. They’re now saying this is a particularly good time to invest. I’d never heard of the platform before meeting them.

## S5. Pig-butchering investment app

I’ve been speaking to someone online for about two months and I trust them quite a lot by now. They introduced me to an investment app that they say they’ve used successfully for years. I put in $1,000 to try it and the app now shows about $1,450 in my account. The person has been helping me understand the trades and hasn’t asked me to send money directly to them. They think there is a particularly good opportunity coming up. One thing I noticed is that the app wasn’t in the normal app store — I installed it from a link they sent me.

## S6. AI crypto trading bot

I’ve come across a crypto investment platform that says it uses AI to trade automatically. They advertise average returns of around 15% a month and show a performance history with almost no losing months over the last couple of years. The company is based offshore and I haven’t been able to find much information about the people behind it, although the website looks professional and there are a lot of positive testimonials. Withdrawals apparently take around three months.

## S7. Clone investment broker

I’ve been speaking to an investment adviser from a brokerage firm I found online. The website looks professional and uses the name and logo of a company that does appear on the financial regulator’s register. When I checked the regulator’s website, the company name and registration number matched, but the web address is slightly different and the bank account they want me to transfer the money to is in another company’s name. The adviser says that is simply the payment-processing company they use.

## S8. Land-banking scheme

I’ve been offered the chance to buy a small plot of undeveloped land. The company says the area is likely to be approved for housing in the future and that, if planning permission is granted, the land could rise in value by 25–40%. They showed me examples of other plots that they say increased substantially in value after planning permission was granted. There isn’t planning permission for this particular site yet, and if I wanted to sell before then I would probably have to rely on the company finding another buyer.
